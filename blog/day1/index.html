Science Has No Borders. Power Does.

科学没有国界，权力有。

If science is universal, why do political boundaries still shape what we can research and which technologies we can use?

Original text 原文

Imagine that you are an engineer who has spent six months building an application around DeepSeek. You have tested its responses, adjusted your prompts, and persuaded your company to adopt it. Then one morning, a government announces that certain organizations can no longer use the technology. Nothing about your code has changed. The model has not suddenly become less intelligent. Yet the system you built may no longer be acceptable. What looked like an engineering decision has turned into a political one.

In October 2026, the United States published an order excluding DeepSeek products from federal civilian agency procurement and requiring their removal from agency systems, including certain third-party products built with DeepSeek. It was not a nationwide ban on private use. But the distinction matters less to a contractor whose product must meet government requirements. For that company, the practical questions are immediate: can another model reproduce the same behavior? Who pays for the migration? And what happens to the months of work that cannot simply be transferred?

This is where the popular idea of technological efficiency becomes incomplete. A model may be cheaper, faster, or more capable, yet still be a risky choice if access to it depends on decisions made outside the engineering team. Switching models is not like changing a light bulb. You may need to redesign your prompts, rebuild evaluation systems, retest safety controls, and renegotiate contracts. A company can reduce this exposure by using interchangeable model interfaces, maintaining alternative providers, and keeping its own evaluation data. These measures cost money. In other words, geopolitical uncertainty has become part of the price of software.

Of course, the security argument cannot simply be dismissed. Governments handle confidential information, and sending it to a foreign service may create real vulnerabilities. A locally deployed model, however, does not necessarily pose the same risks as a remote API. It can still have supply-chain weaknesses or undesirable behavior, but its data flows are different. That is why the scope of a restriction matters. A serious security assessment has to distinguish between where a model was developed, where it runs, what it can access, and who controls it. National origin alone cannot answer all four questions.

TikTok offers another version of this conflict. In a US Senate hearing in January 2024, its Singaporean CEO, Shou Zi Chew, was repeatedly questioned about Chinese citizenship and Communist Party connections. There was a legitimate subject to investigate: the relationship between a platform, its parent company, and the legal system under which that company operates. Yet the questioning also illustrates how easily institutional concerns can become entangled with personal identity. A person's ancestry, citizenship, and actual obligations are three different things. Confusing them does not make a security investigation more rigorous.

The recent episode involving Jensen Huang requires a different distinction. He resigned from Nvidia in November 2026 amid revelations about personal relationships and security concerns, and was subsequently reinstated. The available evidence does not establish that his Chinese ancestry caused his departure. His case instead shows why leaders of strategically important companies face scrutiny that goes beyond ordinary corporate performance. When a company's chips are essential to both commercial innovation and national strategy, its executives' relationships, business decisions, and compliance practices can acquire geopolitical significance.

The consequences extend beyond companies. Research published during and after the US China Initiative has documented anxiety among scientists of Chinese descent. If researchers start avoiding international collaborations because they fear being misunderstood, their decisions may never appear in an official list of restricted projects. A partnership that was never formed, a student who chose not to apply, and an experiment that was never conducted are all difficult to count. This is the chilling effect: the possibility of punishment changes behavior even when no punishment actually occurs. The invisible costs of uncertainty can therefore be much harder to measure than the direct costs of regulation.

But there is a deeper contradiction here. Governments want the economic and strategic advantages of scientific progress. Scientific progress, meanwhile, often depends on people sharing information, criticizing one another, and building on discoveries made elsewhere. Restricting sensitive military technology may be justified in ways that restricting basic mathematical research would not be. The difficulty is deciding where the boundary lies, especially when today's theoretical insight can become tomorrow's commercial or military capability. Artificial intelligence makes this boundary particularly unstable because the same research can serve many different purposes.

So is science really without borders? A mathematical theorem does not become false when it crosses an ocean. But the researchers who prove it need visas, funding, computing resources, and institutions willing to employ them. Scientific truth may be universal; the conditions under which it is produced are not. Perhaps the most useful question is not whether politics can be removed from science altogether. It is whether institutions can protect legitimate security interests without treating international cooperation itself as evidence of danger. That tension will shape not only which countries develop the next generation of technology, but also which discoveries humanity gets to make in the first place.
